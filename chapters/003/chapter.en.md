# The Njål Scale

Before the book, there was the scale.

It was not formally defined. It had no calibration report, no external validator, and no known tolerance for enthusiasm.

It worked roughly like this:

1. Hmm.
2. Interesting.
3. This may be relevant.
4. We should probably test this.
5. EVACUATE THE BUILDING.

The beauty of the scale was that, in practice, all five levels could be expressed as level two.

*Hmm* usually meant a result had behaved incorrectly in a way that might be a flaw in the test.

*Interesting* meant the anomaly had survived its first control.

*This may be relevant* meant the result had reproduced, the control had failed, and a previous explanation had just become substantially less likely.

*We should probably test this* meant a mechanism was beginning to look causal.

*EVACUATE THE BUILDING* meant several hypotheses were dead, the residual signal was still there, and someone needed to remind us that all of this was happening inside a small synthetic world.

The scale did not measure confidence.

It measured how much reality seemed to be changing shape while you were still trying to speak normally.

This caused certain communication problems.

A person might say “interesting” because something was, in fact, interesting.

Njål might say “interesting” after a transplantation had reversed the outcome 189 times without a single failure.

The words were the same.

The information was not.

There was also a method hidden in it.

The feeling of having discovered something and the obligation to try to destroy it can coexist.

Excitement is not evidence.

But the absence of excitement is not integrity, either.

Integrity lies in letting the test win.

When a beautiful explanation falls to a simple control, it should not be rescued by a longer report.

When a surprising result survives, it should not be made smaller out of reflex.

The Njål Scale therefore had one hidden rule:

> The bigger the finding feels, the harder the next test should try to make it trivial.

It is a good rule.

It just has a slightly comic side effect.

If the finding survives, the language has already become so small that nobody outside the room understands what happened.

At that point, one usually says:

> Oh. Exciting. And runs the next test.
